# Editorial and Page Quality for Word

## Reader and voice

Open with the conclusion or decision the reader needs, then supply the minimum evidence
required to trust it. Use descriptive but compact headings, topic sentences that state the
point, and paragraphs with one job. Convert vague claims into specific subject + action +
effect + evidence when the source supports it. Do not manufacture certainty, urgency,
executive approval or numerical precision.

Choose a voice from the artifact, not a generic professional tone:

- Decision briefs: direct, evidence-led, explicit about the decision and unresolved risk.
- Policies: unambiguous, testable and explicit about scope, authority and exceptions.
- SOPs: imperative steps, prerequisites, roles, records, failures and escalation.
- Proposals: distinguish committed deliverables from options and assumptions.
- Research reports: separate finding, method, limitation and recommendation.
- Thought leadership: make a clear argument without slogans or unsupported universals.

Remove throat-clearing, repeated summaries, ornamental adjectives, slogan-like parallelism
and chains of abstract nouns. Preserve agreed terminology and the user's language. Do not
turn every section into a list solely to create visual rhythm.

## Tables, captions and lists

A table should answer a comparison or lookup question. Give it a clear header, state units
and periods, align numbers consistently, order rows by a meaningful key and move caveats to
concise notes. Avoid copying body paragraphs into narrow cells. Captions should identify
what the table or figure shows and, when useful, the interpretation.

Lists use parallel grammar and one idea per item. Ordered lists are for sequence, priority
or instruction. Unordered lists are for peer items. Do not split one sentence into decorative
bullets.

## Design system

For new reports, set a coherent `design` object before generating:

- `executive`: board and operating reviews with teal/charcoal restraint.
- `consulting`: evidence-heavy analysis and recommendations with navy/slate contrast.
- `editorial`: proposals and narrative briefs with warm paper and brick/teal signals.
- `minimal`: policies, technical notes and long reference documents.

`airy`, `balanced` and `compact` control vertical rhythm. Use `design.cover_page:true` only
when a substantial document benefits from a title page. Keep the accent functional and
limited to hierarchy, selected table headers, links or one emphasis per page. Do not combine
preset colors with unrelated manual colors unless an approved brand requires it.

Section rhythm should alternate purposefully: assertion-led heading and body, a compact
evidence table, a callout for the single most important implication, then a short action
list. Use callouts sparingly. A page with only one short paragraph is usually under-designed;
a page with many unrelated boxes is over-designed.

Use a native `callout` for a decision guardrail, risk, principle or next action that must be
visually separable from the body. The title should state the implication; the text should
state the evidence or action. Do not repeat an adjacent paragraph inside the callout.

## Final editorial pass

Read the document as three artifacts: a decision aid, a piece of prose and a paginated
object. Verify that every section answers the brief, every table is necessary, the visual
hierarchy matches importance, terminology and numbers agree across sections, and the final
page leaves the reader with a clear decision or next action. Inspect the rendered page for
widows, orphans, awkward page breaks, repeated headings, clipped tables and unreadable
footnotes. Semantic text extraction is not a pagination review.
